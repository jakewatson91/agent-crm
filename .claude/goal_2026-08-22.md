# Where this is actually stuck, measured

2026-08-22. Everything below was measured on Sudden (`e7052848`) and the live
database today, except the market numbers, which are sourced at the bottom.

## The short version

You are right that the drafts are not good enough and right that something is
too complicated. You have the cause backwards on both, and the good news is that
the real cause is much smaller than the one you are worried about.

**The drafts are not failing on craft. They are failing on the argument.** Sudden
has written 85 drafts in its life and 76 of them, 89%, were withdrawn for one
reason: the message worked out the wrong reason to write. Your own notes on the ones you kept
say the same thing. "DO NOT use spikes. Our pitch is on back catalogue. Not new
shows." That is not a writing problem, and no number of writing rules would have
caught it.

**The rules are not what stops a customer customising, because no customer ever
sees them.** A new workspace gets 18,408 characters of writing instruction and
contributes 14% of the finished prompt from its own words. What actually stops a
customer fixing a bad message is that until this week the only things they could
change were about thirty numbers about scoring and a paragraph describing their
company, and the thing that goes wrong is neither of those.

**Do not push the human further out.** The human is already the fastest part of
the loop. From the moment something happens at a company to the moment you decide
yes or no takes about 13 days, and you account for 1.9 of them. The machine takes
the other 11.2. Meanwhile a message sent inside 48 hours of the event gets three
to five times the reply rate, and only 8% of these go out that fast. Taking
yourself out of the loop buys 1.9 days out of 13 and costs the one signal that
makes the next message better.

## The rules

For a brand new workspace with nothing configured, the drafter prompt is 18,408
characters. Nine numbered steps, 30 bulleted rules, 22 places where it says never
or must not. Sudden's live version is 26,486 characters.

Where it goes:

| Block | Chars | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Line editing (weld it into one voice, punctuation, reading level) | 5,794 | 31.5% |
| Write the question they can answer from memory | 2,209 | 12.0% |
| Explaining the shortlist of facts it is allowed to cite | 2,184 | 11.9% |
| Turn the event into the job they hate | 1,744 | 9.5% |
| How the message ends | 1,327 | 7.2% |
| The checklist at the end | 1,261 | 6.9% |
| The shape of the message | 1,117 | 6.1% |
| Credibility and pitch | 926 | 5.0% |
| Read the event you were given | 874 | 4.7% |
| Length target | 798 | 4.3% |

Here is where I have to correct my own table. The 2,184-character row is
mislabelled: it measures from the shortlist note to the end of the prompt, and
most of that is the refusal instruction, the reasoning field, the citation format
and the JSON schema, all of which are load bearing. The shortlist note itself is
983 characters.

Redoing the count honestly, the block that clearly restates a decision already
made and handed over is STEP 1, at 874 characters, which says what the user
prompt's own anchor block says a second time and less forcefully. That is 4.6%
of the prompt, not a third. STEP 9's closing checklist, another 1,261 characters,
repeats four earlier steps, but a final self-check is a real technique and I have
no evidence that removing it is free.

So: the redundancy is real and it is about 5% that I can point at, not 31%. The
way to find the rest is to delete one block and dry-run the same accounts through
both versions, one block at a time. That is a measurement job, not an edit.

That pattern repeats through the file, and the code says so itself, in its own
comments:

- "that rule was written three times and lost to the exemplar all three times"
- "STEP 0 has carried that paragraph since 2026-08-13 and three of four drafts
  anchored on a ruled-out fact anyway"
- "the craft rules moved banned CTAs to zero and then 17 of 27 drafts closed on
  one of the menu's own two example sentences instead. The rules did not stop the
  copying, they changed what got copied."

Every time a rule failed, the fix that worked was moving the decision out of the
prompt and into code. Pick the event in code. Match the argument in code. Drop the
facts about parts of the business you cannot serve before the model sees them. Cut
the example message's body so there is nothing to copy. Those all held. The prose
versions of the same rules all lost. What nobody did afterwards was delete the
prose version once the real fix shipped, so the prompt is now a record of every
failed attempt stacked on top of the one that worked.

So the maintenance problem is real and it is not the one you named. It is not too
many rules for a customer to manage. It is that nobody can tell
which rules are load bearing, because the ones that lost were never deleted after
the fix that won shipped.

### I tested whether the rest of it earns its keep

Same account, same event, same argument, same model. One draft from the live
26,486-character prompt, one from a 4,841-character prompt that keeps the
company description, the argument, and about ten rules a model cannot infer
(never invent a number, never name an internal field, no links, never ask for
time, write to the company if you have no contact name).

Two accounts finished before I wrote this.

**Apple TV+**, on the Stillwater season five premiere. The live prompt took 107
seconds, spent 6,370 output tokens and produced 381 characters. It made the right
argument and closed on "Can I work out your catalogue number?", which is the exact
construction you banned on 08-09. The short prompt took 68 seconds, spent 4,214
output tokens and produced 792 characters. Same right argument, cleanly, but it
asked "how much of the following weeks' viewing lands on the earlier seasons",
and the long prompt explicitly forbids a question starting with "how much" because
it makes them go and look something up.

**Warner Brothers Discovery**, on the Banksters announcement. The live prompt took
87 seconds, 5,005 output tokens, 380 characters, and closed on "Should I estimate
the Banksters catch-up saving?", the same ask-permission ending again. The short
prompt took 49 seconds, 2,934 output tokens, 701 characters, and closed on the ask
itself: "Put the back catalog on us and leave the Banksters premiere exactly as it
is."

Across the two, the short prompt ran 40% faster and spent 37% fewer output tokens
for the same argument. Neither version broke any of the mechanical checks: no
banned call to action, no filler verbs, no telling the reader what they feel, no
dashes.

That is an honest split rather than a clean win, and it is more useful than a
clean win would have been. The line-editing rules do buy something real: the short
prompt asked a question the long one would have caught. They also cost five and a
half times the prompt, spend half again the reasoning, and they still let the same
weak ending through twice in two tries. One caveat on my test: neither version was
given your past edits, which the live system does pass in. So the short prompt was
running without the correction that would most likely have caught its mistake, and
the long one was running without the correction that should have caught its.

### The "how much" ban was wrong, and it is gone

You pushed back on the rule that made the short prompt's question a failure, and
you were right. Two reasons, one of them sitting inside the prompt already.

STEP 3 banned any question opening with how much, how big, where or what share,
on the grounds that they have to go and look it up and the honest reply is
silence. STEP 4, four lines later, says the strongest credibility move is naming
a cost they cannot currently see. Asking about a number they do not have is the
sharpest way to do that, so the prompt banned the shape it recommends. Sudden's
own approved drafts make the move: "the repeat share is a cost most can't see"
went out on 07-31.

The failure the rule was aimed at is real but narrower than the ban. It is a
question that assumes they already have the figure, so "I would have to check"
reads as a confession and they say nothing. The fix is to ask whether they can
SEE the thing rather than what its value is. "Does that show up broken out
anywhere, or does it just sit inside the total?" is answerable from memory by
anyone who works there, and a no is the interesting answer.

That is what the rule says now. Worth noting the live prompt's own draft passed
this test and the short one did not: "Is that back catalogue part of the launch's
CDN budget, or does it just hit the bill as it comes?" against "how much of the
following weeks' viewing lands on the earlier seasons". The revision keeps what
made the first one better and drops the blanket ban.

One correction to my own framing: I led that comparison on speed, and you are
right that speed is not a cost you care about. Strike it. What is left is that
the short prompt spent 37% fewer output tokens and ran longer than the character
target, and the long prompt held the length and still produced an ending you had
already banned.

The conclusion is not "delete the writing rules". It is that they are worth maybe
a tight checklist, and the argument is worth everything, and right now the
proportions are the other way round.

## How much human, and where

This is the question I think you have most wrong, and the numbers are clean.

I traced every draft back to the event it was written about:

| Step | Median |
|---|---|
| The thing happens at the company, and we find out | 4.9 days |
| We know it, and a draft gets written | 6.3 days |
| The draft exists, and you decide | 1.9 days |
| **Event to decision** | **~13 days** |

Only 8% of drafts go out inside two days of the event. 24% inside a week. The
oldest thing still sitting in your approval queue has been there 9 days, and there
are 40 waiting.

I thought I had found the cause of the middle row, and checking it properly
before building anything showed I had not. Writing the correction down because
the wrong version is the more attractive story.

What I first saw: 12, 12 and 14 drafts on three recent days against a cap of 12,
which reads as a queue backing up behind a ceiling. What the pass's own run log
says is that those came from **two manual runs**, not from the daily schedule.
The scheduled runs either side of them produced 0 and 1 drafts and never came
near the cap:

```
2026-08-19 14:33  cron    drafts= 0   cap reached=  0
2026-08-19 17:47  manual  drafts=12   cap reached= 57
2026-08-20 14:38  cron    drafts= 1   cap reached=  0
2026-08-21 15:29  manual  drafts=12   cap reached= 51
2026-08-22 15:13  manual  drafts= 6   cap reached=  0
```

So the cap is not what is costing six days today. The ordering point still
stands, because on the two runs that did have supply, 51 and 57 accounts were
turned away and the comparator that chose between them never looks at how old
the event is. It is worth fixing as the thing that bites the moment supply comes
back. It is not the six days.

**What the log says is actually blocking, on the most recent run:**

| Reason the pass wrote nothing | Accounts |
|---|---|
| Did not clear the scoring bars | 313 |
| Anchor too weak to build a message on | 38 |
| Argument written but never confirmed, so it stops after 3 drafts | 17 |
| No configured argument fits them | 24 |
| Already messaged recently | 6 |

The two argument rows are 41 accounts a run, and until today neither had any
surface at all. The confirmation is a checkbox three levels into Settings, and
the "no argument fits" case was recorded in a tally nothing reads.

Published benchmarks for 2026 put a signal-triggered cold email at 5% to 18% reply
against 1% to 3% for a generic one, and a message sent within 24 to 48 hours of
the trigger at three to five times the response rate of the same message sent
later. Meanwhile average cold email reply rates fell from 5.1% in 2024 to 3.43%
in 2026, and 50% to 70% of AI SDR tools get cancelled inside their first year,
because buyers have learned to recognise the output.

Put those together and the answer to "90-95% agent or more human in the writing"
is that it is the wrong axis. You are not choosing between the human writing and
the machine writing. You are choosing what the human touches:

**What the human should touch, once:** the argument. When this happens at a
company, and this other thing is true about them, here is what it costs them and
here is what I want them to do about it. Written once, applied across the whole
book. The editor for this already exists and does the right thing: an argument the
setup flow guessed gets three drafts and then stops until a person reads those
three and confirms it. That is the human doing one hour of work that governs a
thousand messages.

**What the human should touch, every time:** yes or no, and an edit when it is
wrong. Your edits are the highest-value data in the system. They already flow into
the next draft's context as corrections, over a 90-day window, five at a time.
Removing you from that loop does not save time you care about, and it switches off
the only thing that makes the drafts better next month than this month.

**What the human should never touch:** the thirty numbers in Settings. Score
needed to draft. Evidence depth needed to draft. Minimum ICP-match strength to
count as evidence. Nobody with five minutes a day is tuning those, and none of
them has ever been the reason a message was wrong.

## What makes it undeniable

The comparison you named is the right one, and it is not hypothetical any more.
Apollo, Amplemarket and Breakcold all ship MCP servers, so a founder can point
Claude at a prospect database and run their morning inside a chat window. Claude
Cowork got projects that persist between sessions in late March. That is your
competitor, not HubSpot.

Three things a chat session structurally cannot do, and this can:

**It reads all of them, every day, while you are asleep.** A founder in a chat
window looks at maybe twenty companies in a sitting, and only the ones they
thought to name. This holds facts on 2,243 companies and researched 640 of them
last month. The founder does not know which of their two thousand accounts had
something happen yesterday, and cannot find out by asking.

**It never says the same thing twice.** Every fact ever cited to an account is
recorded and put out of bounds for the next message. A fresh chat session has no
idea what you already sent, and will happily send the same angle again.

**It gets better from your corrections and a chat forgets them.** Your rewrites
are stored against the account and fed to the next draft. Every new chat session
starts from nothing.

The strongest of those is the first one combined with speed, and it converts
straight into the sentence a buyer feels:

> Something happened at one of your two thousand accounts yesterday, and there is
> an email about it waiting for you this morning.

That is a thing a founder with Claude cannot do at any price without hiring
someone. It is also, today, not true: the median is 13 days, not overnight. The
distance between that sentence and the current build is the whole product, and it
is a build problem, not a positioning problem.

Price supports it. Unify is $1,740 a month. Clay is $185 to $495 plus credits.
Sudden's full bill last month, searches and models and everything, was under $50.

## What to do, in order

Reordered after the checks above. Steps 1 and 2 are done and committed; the rest
is what the evidence now supports.

1. **DONE, say out loud when an argument is holding the book back.** Two new
   amber notices on Today, both read from the pass's own tally so they can never
   disagree with what actually happened. One fires when an unconfirmed argument
   has hit its three-draft limit, and links straight to the confirm control.
   The other fires when accounts clear every bar and no argument fits them, which
   is the system telling the customer what their arguments do not cover. On Sudden
   right now they read "17 companies are waiting on you to confirm an argument"
   and "24 companies cleared every bar and got no message".

2. **DONE, write to the freshest event first.** `compareWalkOrder` now ranks by
   the age of the anchor, above signal strength and fit and below the bars, and
   `loadAnchoredAccounts` carries the freshest anchor date instead of a yes/no.
   Twelve new assertions in `check_advance_order.ts` pin it, including that
   freshness never overrides the bars and that a junk date cannot poison the sort.
   Framed honestly: this is insurance for the days that have supply, not the six
   days.

3. **Confirm the catalogue argument, or write the second one.** This is yours, not
   mine. 17 accounts are held pending your read of three drafts, and 24 more have
   nothing the single configured argument can honestly say about them. One
   argument is not enough for a book of two thousand.

4. **Then go after the 4.9 days it takes to find out at all.** The market sweep
   design in `.claude/design_market_sweep.md` is for exactly this. Its success
   measure is that the median age of the anchor at draft time falls from weeks to
   days, and it notes nobody has the baseline. The baseline is 12.7 days median,
   8% inside 48 hours. It exists now.

5. **Find the real redundant share of the prompt by measuring, not by reading.**
   Delete one block, dry-run the same accounts through both versions, keep the
   deletion if nothing changes. STEP 1 is the one to try first, at 874 characters.
   Do not do a sweeping cut on the strength of a character count.

6. **Stop tuning writing rules against single bad drafts.** Every rule added that
   way has lost. When a draft is wrong, ask first whether the argument was wrong,
   second whether the event was wrong, and only third whether the sentence was
   wrong. Only the third is a prompt change.

7. **Hide the thirty numbers.** They are derived at setup and they work. Nobody
   with five minutes a day should be looking at "Minimum ICP-match strength to
   count as evidence".
